## Federal / Cross-State

### DEA Telehealth Prescribing Extension (COVID-era rules)
- **Status**: Extended through **December 31, 2026** (final extension per DEA; no further extensions expected).
- **Impact**: Med spas offering GLP-1, HRT, testosterone, or other controlled-substance prescribing via telehealth may continue under the COVID-era flexibilities through year-end 2026. Practices must plan for the transition to in-person-first prescribing beginning January 1, 2027.
- **Skill hook**: `sales/membership-program-builder` — any clinical-tier membership that includes a telehealth-prescribing component must flag the 2026 year-end deadline so the program is structured to survive the transition.

### HIPAA (ongoing)
- HIPAA violations can reach $50,000 per violation; annual fines can top $2 million. Cash-pay practices are not exempt.
- 2026 standard: encrypted electronic health records, limited user access controls, audit trails, regular risk assessments, multi-factor authentication.
- **Skill hook**: `ai-consent-and-compliance-guardrails`, `cancellation-no-show-policy-author` (PHI-aware SMS language), `membership-program-builder` (HIPAA-aware enrollment copy — no clinical service names in external SMS).

### TCPA / FCC — AI-Generated Voice Calls and Texts (NEW — added 2026-07-13 monitor cycle)
- **Status**: The federal telephone-consumer regime already reaches AI voice. A **February 2024 FCC declaratory ruling** confirmed that the TCPA's restrictions on an "artificial or prerecorded voice" **encompass AI-generated voices**, meaning calls placed with an AI voice require the called party's **prior express consent** on the same footing as any other artificial-voice call. A subsequent **FCC Notice of Proposed Rulemaking** (opened August 2024) would go further: formally define an "AI-generated call," require a **plain-language in-call AI disclosure at the opening of the call**, and require consent language that **specifically names AI use**. As of this cycle the NPRM is **still not finalized** — treat the disclosure requirement as directional-but-not-yet-binding at the federal level, while noting that the *consent* requirement for AI voice is already live via the 2024 ruling. Statutory damages run **up to $500 per violation, trebled to $1,500 for willful or knowing violations**, with **no aggregate statutory cap** (some courts have trimmed constitutionally excessive aggregate awards on due-process grounds, so "uncapped" is true statutorily rather than absolutely). Per-call damages with no statutory ceiling are what make this the **highest-dollar exposure in the AI-communications stack** for a small practice — and unlike the state AI-disclosure layer, it is **already live**, not proposed.
- **Impact for salons / spas / med spas**: This is the **US counterpart to the EU Art. 50(1) chatbot-disclosure duty**, and it is the gate that actually bites for the ~all-domestic practices this repo mostly serves — a fact the repo's prior AI-disclosure coverage (EU Art. 50 + CA AB-489 only) did not address. It fires hardest on **outbound** AI voice and text: appointment reminders, no-show follow-ups, waitlist gap-fill calls, win-back campaigns, and any AI voice agent that *places* calls rather than only answering them. Two operational consequences: (1) a generic marketing opt-in that says only "you may receive automated calls/texts" may **not** carry the load under the proposed AI-naming standard — the opt-in should name **AI-generated voice** explicitly before an outbound voice agent is switched on; (2) the AI disclosure and a working opt-out belong in the **opening seconds** of the call, engineered into the script architecture rather than appended. Inbound-only receptionists (client called you) sit in a materially lighter posture than outbound agents — the distinction should be preserved in every script.
- **Skill hook**: `operations/ai-receptionist-disclosure-and-escalation` (**new this cycle** — owns the opening-disclosure line, the outbound consent/opt-out/calling-window block, and the AI-voice-naming dependency on the opt-in form); `operations/ai-consent-and-compliance-guardrails` (Artifact 2 opt-in language should name AI-generated voice where the practice runs, or plans to run, an outbound AI voice agent); `sales/sms-campaign-builder`, `operations/no-show-risk-reminder`, `operations/waitlist-gap-fill-outreach`, `customer-service/client-winback-sequence`, `customer-service/treatment-cadence-rebooking` (all generate copy an AI agent may deliver outbound — the campaign skill owns the offer, the receptionist skill owns the opening seconds).

### State AI-Disclosure and Call-Recording Layer (NEW — added 2026-07-13 monitor cycle)
- **Status**: A distinct, **state-level** layer sitting on top of the federal regime above, parts of which are **already binding** while the FCC's disclosure rule is not. **Verify each state with counsel — this layer is moving fast and is badly reported.**
- **⚠️ Sourcing warning (recorded deliberately, 2026-07-13)**: this cycle's first draft of this entry asserted that *Texas SB 140 requires AI-voice disclosure within the first 30 seconds of a call and restricts voice cloning.* **That claim is false.** It appears widely in **AI-vendor marketing blogs and voice-agent SEO content**, which cite each other in a closed loop with no primary-source or law-firm backing. The actual SB 140 (effective **2025-09-01**) is a **telemarketing** law with no AI provision at all (see the Texas entry below). The error was caught by this cycle's verification pass. **Standing rule for future cycles: never source a regulatory fact from an AI-tooling vendor's blog. Confirm against the statute, a state agency, or a law-firm alert before it enters this KB.**
- **What is actually verified**: state AI-disclosure duties **do** exist but are **narrower than the vendor press implies** — they attach mostly to *government agencies* and to *healthcare practitioners using AI for diagnostic or treatment purposes* (see **Texas SB 1188** and **TRAIGA/HB 149** in the Texas entry below), **not** to ordinary private-sector booking chatbots as a general class. A salon's or med spa's booking bot is, in most states today, **not** subject to a state statutory AI-disclosure mandate. Disclose anyway — see the "why disclose regardless" note below — but do not let a skill claim a legal duty that does not exist.
- **Call-recording consent is the layer that actually bites**: essentially every AI voice agent **transcribes by default**, which puts the practice inside its state's recording-consent regime (**all-party vs. one-party**). This is the element most commonly missed in vendor-default greeting scripts, and unlike the AI-disclosure duty it applies to every practice with a voice agent, everywhere. A practice taking calls from clients in multiple states cannot rely on its own state's rule — the bot does not know where the caller is sitting, so the script should be built to the **strictest applicable standard**. Route to counsel for the practice's specific states.
- **Why disclose regardless of a state mandate**: (a) the **EU** Art. 50(1) duty is real and applicable **2026-08-02** for any EU-facing practice; (b) the **FCC NPRM** would make in-call AI disclosure binding federally if finalized; (c) **CA AB-489** already bars an AI system from implying licensed-professional care, which a non-disclosing bot with a human name and a clinical register walks straight into; (d) undisclosed bots are a consumer-protection/deception exposure independent of any AI-specific statute. Disclosure is cheap; the exposure is not.
- **Skill hook**: `operations/ai-receptionist-disclosure-and-escalation` (Rule 1 disclosure — framed as **best practice + EU/CA-driven**, not as a universal US mandate; Rule 2 recording-consent line with a real path to decline; multi-state → strictest-standard rule); `operations/ai-consent-and-compliance-guardrails` (TX healthcare-AI-diagnostic gate alongside the existing CA/CO/IN gates).

### EU AI Act Article 50 — AI-Generated Content Transparency
- **Applicability date**: **August 2, 2026** — now under two weeks out (13 days as of the 2026-07-20 cycle). The transitional period to **December 2, 2026** applies **only** to the Art. 50(2) machine-marking/detection obligation for AI-generated content, and **only** for systems placed on the market before 2026-08-02; the Art. 50(1) (interaction disclosure), 50(3) (emotion/biometric), and 50(4) (deepfake/public-interest-text) duties are live 2026-08-02 with **no grace period** (per the Commission Art. 50 FAQ, 2026-07-20).
- **⭐ Status update (2026-07-20 monitor cycle) — RESOLVED, and it corrects a prior framing error**: The European Commission **ADOPTED and PUBLISHED the FINAL Article 50 guidelines on 2026-07-20** (*Guidelines on transparency obligations for providers and deployers of AI systems*, plus the Commission communication approving their content). This closes the "final guidelines pending" watch item that this file has carried since the 2026-05-08 draft. The guidelines are now the authoritative text on Art. 50 scope, definitions, obligations, and exceptions. **Verified against the primary source** (digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu library page + the official Art. 50 Q&A, both dated 2026-07-20), per the standing anti-vendor-blog sourcing rule. **Key correction the final guidance forces on this repo's earlier framing:** the Art. 50(1) "you are interacting with AI" disclosure duty is written as a **PROVIDER** obligation — it binds whoever *develops the chatbot and places it on the market under their own name/trademark*, i.e. the booking-bot **vendor**, not the salon that switches it on. A salon running a third-party booking/receptionist bot is a **deployer**, and a deployer's *own* direct Art. 50 duties are narrower: **50(3)** (inform people exposed to emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation systems) and **50(4)** (label deepfakes, and label AI-generated/manipulated *text published to inform the public on matters of public interest*). Practical upshot: (a) the salon should **confirm its chatbot vendor built the 50(1) disclosure in** rather than assume it must add it, but (b) a salon that **white-labels, self-brands, or self-builds** a bot under its own name can itself become the **provider** and inherit the 50(1) duty directly, and (c) the salon remains on the hook as **deployer** for 50(3)/(4) regardless. This is a genuine refinement of the prior line that called 50(1) "the headline operator duty" — accurate as a description of *what the bot must do*, imprecise about *who the Act names as legally responsible*. The four cumulative 50(1) criteria (must be an AI system; a genuine two-way exchange; direct, not via a human intermediary; with natural persons) and the restrictively-interpreted "**obvious**" exception (judged by a reasonably well-informed, observant average person) are now fixed in the final text. **Fines**: up to **€15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover**, whichever is greater, with **proportionality for SMEs/small mid-caps** — a small salon is squarely in the SME-proportionality band. **Enforcement**: national market-surveillance authorities (the AI Office's role is limited to GPAI-built systems). The **2026-07-22 signatory-form date** for the separate Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content remains a *signatory* deadline, not an operator compliance deadline — unchanged, and still generally not something a small deployer needs to act on.
- **Superseded status note (2026-07-13 monitor cycle)**: (retained for history) The final Art. 50 **guidelines** were **PENDING** as of the 2026-07-13 cycle — draft published 2026-05-08, consultation closed 2026-06-03. **Now resolved: final guidelines published 2026-07-20 (see above).** New operational detail this cycle: the Commission has set a **2026-07-22 deadline for signatory forms** to the (separate, already-final) Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content, with the list of **initial signatories to be published before the Art. 50 obligations take effect**. Signing is voluntary and gives a presumption of compliance; a salon/spa is a *deployer*, and the deployer-facing Section 2 duties are narrow (deepfakes + public-interest text), so **signing is generally not something a small practice needs to do** — the date is logged so the repo does not mistake a signatory deadline for a compliance deadline. The operator-facing duty remains **Art. 50(1) chatbot disclosure**, and that duty's applicability date (2026-08-02) is unchanged and does not depend on the guidelines publishing first.
- **Status (as of June 22, 2026)**: Two distinct Commission instruments, at different stages. (1) The **FINAL Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content was PUBLISHED by the AI Office on 2026-06-10** (resolved the watch item carried since the 2026-06-08 cycle). The Code is voluntary; signing the relevant chapter gives a presumption of compliance, and future enforcement will focus on adherence. Structure to note for skill purposes — the Code has **two sections only**: Section 1 = **provider** obligations under Art. 50(2) (machine-readable marking/detection of AI audio/image/video/text); Section 2 = **deployer** obligations under Art. 50(4) (labelling of *deepfakes* and AI-generated/manipulated *text published to inform the public on matters of public interest*), with an optional EU label icon in three variants (Annex I). (2) **Separately, the Commission's Article 50 *guidelines* — which cover the Art. 50(1) "you are interacting with AI" chatbot-disclosure duty and the scope questions — were published in DRAFT on 2026-05-08; the stakeholder consultation closed 2026-06-03 and the FINAL guidelines remain pending, expected ahead of the 2026-08-02 applicability date** (2026-06-22 monitor note). That chatbot-disclosure duty, not the Code, is the part most operators' AI booking chatbots actually trip. The draft guidelines sharpen the duty in two operator-relevant ways: the "this is AI" disclosure must be made **at the moment of first interaction** (it cannot be buried in terms-of-service, a settings menu, or a privacy policy), and the duty **extends to "agentic" systems** that take actions on a client's behalf (e.g., an AI agent that books, reschedules, or cancels). Treat the draft as strong directional guidance but not yet the final authoritative text.
- **Impact for salons/spas (deployer view, scoped — updated 2026-07-20)**: The practical outcome a client experiences is unchanged — an EU-client-facing AI booking/receptionist bot must make clear the client is talking to AI (Art. 50(1)) — but the **legal ownership** of that duty sits with the bot's **provider/vendor**, so the salon's job is to *verify the disclosure ships enabled* and to *not disable it*, plus to be aware that self-branding or self-building a bot pulls the 50(1) duty onto the salon directly. The salon's own **deployer** duties are 50(3) and 50(4). The Art. 50(4) *labelling* duty is **narrow**: it bites on **deepfakes** and on AI text "on matters of public interest" — ordinary AI-drafted booking confirmations, promo emails, social captions, and intake copy are generally **outside** that public-interest trigger (a promotional caption is not public-interest text), so blanket "this message was written by AI" labels on routine transactional/marketing copy are **not** required by 50(4), and human review exempts text from 50(4) in any case. Don't over-label routine copy. **One med-spa-specific 50(4) deepfake exposure to flag**: AI-generated or AI-simulated *before/after* imagery that resembles a real, identifiable person and could be taken as an authentic result photo can meet the deepfake definition — the **deployer** must disclose/label such content on first exposure, and cannot rely on the provider's machine-readable mark alone to satisfy that. AI-generated *images* in marketing may also carry machine-readable provenance marks set by the image **provider** (Art. 50(2) is a provider duty), separate from the salon's 50(4) label.
- **Skill hook**: `operations/ai-consent-and-compliance-guardrails` Artifact 4 (EU booking-confirmation notice) — **fully UNBLOCKED as of 2026-07-20**: both the final Code of Practice (published 2026-06-10) and now the **final Art. 50 guidelines (published 2026-07-20)** are the authoritative text, so nothing is left pending. Hand-off to skill-evaluator, updated: (a) frame the **Art. 50(1) chatbot-disclosure** duty accurately — the bot must disclose at first interaction, but the *legal duty-holder is the provider/vendor*; the deployer-salon's guidance should be "confirm your vendor's bot discloses and don't disable it; if you self-brand/self-build, you become the provider and own the duty"; (b) narrow any standing "AI-generated content" labelling guidance so it does not over-claim a 50(4) obligation on routine transactional/marketing copy (50(4) = deepfakes + public-interest text only; human-reviewed text is exempt); (c) **add a med-spa 50(4) deepfake line** for AI-simulated before/after imagery resembling a real person (deployer must label on first exposure); (d) note the optional EU label icon (Annex I) as available-but-not-required; (e) note that the 2026-12-02 transitional cutoff applies **only** to the 50(2) marking duty for pre-existing systems, not to 50(1)/(3)/(4); (f) note SME/small-mid-cap fine proportionality. The final guidelines confirm the "obvious"-interaction exception is read **restrictively** (reasonably-informed average-person test) and fix the four cumulative 50(1) criteria — cite the final guidelines, not the May draft, as the authoritative source.

### California
- **AB-890 (effective 2026)**: Nurse practitioners with six or more years of experience, or who hold a Doctor of Nursing Practice (DNP) degree, may own and operate a med spa without physician supervision. Significant change in a state that has historically been strict on Corporate Practice of Medicine (CPOM).
- **AB-489 — Health care professions: deceptive terms or letters: artificial intelligence (effective January 1, 2026)**: Prohibits AI systems (including generative AI used for marketing or client-facing communication) from using terms, post-nominal letters, phrases, or design elements that indicate or imply the speaker is, or has the qualifications of, a licensed health care professional, unless the content is genuinely supervised by an in-state licensee. Captures marketing language like "doctor-level," "clinician-guided," "expert-backed," "MD-formulated," and similar when used by an AI system without genuine licensed-pro involvement. Enforcement: the applicable health-care professional licensing board may seek injunctive or other authorized remedies, and each use of a restricted term is a separate violation.
  - **Practice impact for salons / med spas in CA**: any AI-drafted marketing email, SMS, website copy, social caption, or chatbot reply that touches a clinical service (injectables, lasers, dermaplaning, IPL, microneedling, IV hydration, medical-grade skincare claims) must be reviewed for license-implication terms before send. Front-desk AI receptionist scripts and chatbot fallbacks need an explicit "no clinical advice" stop rule. The skill `operations/ai-consent-and-compliance-guardrails` should treat AB-489 as a hard gate for the CA marketing-copy and chatbot-script artifacts; the skill `customer-service/review-response-writer` must avoid drafting "clinician-approved" / "doctor-recommended" replies on CA-state med-spa accounts unless the medical director has signed off.
  - **Enforcement texture (June 2026 monitor note)**: legal-press reporting this cycle describes boards and regulators increasingly using automated "crawler audits" — programmatic sweeps of a practice's public web, social, and booking copy — to flag practices whose marketing language outpaces their actual licensed-supervision posture. The operational takeaway for skills: AB-489 exposure is not limited to a single send; the *standing* public surface (website service pages, evergreen social, AI-chatbot fallbacks) is the more likely first point of a board crawler hit. Skills that generate persistent public copy (`sales/social-caption-writer`, `sales/social-content-calendar-planner`, `_shared/email-drafter` when used for evergreen web copy) should apply the restricted-term checklist to standing copy, not just one-off campaigns.
- **SB 351 — Patient-Specific Orders + Good Faith Exam (2026 enforcement standard)**: SB 351, together with intensified Medical Board oversight, has effectively ended the use of standing protocols as a substitute for individualized clinical authorization. The 2026 enforcement standard expects that **before any medical treatment** (injectables, lasers, radiofrequency, IPL, IV therapy, peptides, compounded medications) every patient receives an individualized, documented **Patient-Specific Order (PSO)** issued by the responsible clinician *after a documented Good Faith Exam* of that specific patient (in-person or via compliant telehealth). Treating off a blanket protocol without an individualized order is reported as the single most common chart deficiency in 2026 California med-spa inspections.
  - **Practice impact for CA med spas**: the booking and intake flow must not promise, schedule-to-treat, or imply same-visit clinical service delivery without routing through a Good Faith Exam and PSO first. Any AI-drafted intake, consultation, or booking-confirmation copy for a CA clinical-tier service must preserve the "treatment is subject to clinician assessment and a patient-specific order" gate and must never state or imply that a treatment is pre-approved. This is a *consultation-flow* compliance gate, distinct from the AB-489 *language* gate.
  - **Skill hook**: `customer-service/client-consultation-intake` and `customer-service/virtual-consultation-intake` (CA clinical-tier intake must carry the GFE/PSO-before-treatment gate and avoid pre-approval language); `customer-service/booking-confirmation-sequence` (CA clinical-tier confirmations state the assessment-first condition); `operations/ai-consent-and-compliance-guardrails` (CA PSO/Good-Faith-Exam consultation gate alongside the AB-489 language gate); `operations/client-consultation-notes` (CA documentation note: PSO is the chart artifact inspectors check).
- **Membership refund-grace window**: California Business & Professions Code §8599 and related provisions — health club and certain wellness service contracts require a **3–7 day right of rescission** depending on the contract type. Route to attorney for the exact applicable section for med spa memberships.
- **CPOM rule**: Physicians and now NPs (post-AB-890) may own the medical entity. Non-physician ownership structures require careful MSO/PC setup.
- **Skill hook**: `sales/membership-program-builder` (California refund-grace block); `cancellation-no-show-policy-author` (CA refund-grace for pre-paid series); `ai-consent-and-compliance-guardrails` (ownership structure compliance note **plus the AB-489 license-term hard gate**); `customer-service/review-response-writer` (AB-489 clinical-language guard); `sales/social-caption-writer` and `sales/sms-campaign-builder` (AB-489 restricted-term checklist for CA-state clinical-tier services).

### Colorado
- **HB 1024** (effective **August 5, 2025**): Med spas must prominently display their cancellation, refund, and membership terms in the digital booking flow and at the physical treatment location.
- **SB 26-189 — Colorado AI Act replacement (signed May 14, 2026; effective January 1, 2027)**: Repeals and replaces the 2024 Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205, which had been scheduled to take effect June 30, 2026). Reorients the framework around developers and deployers of "Covered Automated Decision-Making Technology" used to materially influence a consequential decision in covered domains (health care services among them). Imposes documentation, developer-notification-of-material-update, and deployer-risk-management duties; backs off some of the broader 2024-Act obligations. **HIPAA-covered entities and their business associates are largely exempt** from the new law's main duties outside of consequential-employment and financial-assistance decisions. FDA-regulated medical devices and pharmaceutical R&D are excluded from coverage entirely.
  - **Practice impact for CO med spas**: cash-pay aesthetic practices that are *not* HIPAA-covered (e.g., some pure cosmetic-only spas with no medical record-keeping) lose the broad HIPAA carve-out and may have deployer duties for any AI tool that materially influences a "consequential decision" — for med spas, the most likely capture is AI-driven hiring tools and AI-driven financing-eligibility tools, not AI-drafted marketing copy. Skills should not assume CO med spas are automatically exempt; the exempt status depends on whether the practice is HIPAA-covered.
  - **Status note**: this is a separate framework from HB 1024 (which is a consumer-disclosure rule for med-spa cancellation terms). The two coexist.
- **SB 26-130 — "Medical Spas Deceptive Trade Practices" / Medical Spa Prescription Drug Consumer Protection Act — DIED IN COMMITTEE (status correction, 2026-06-22 monitor cycle)**: Per the Colorado General Assembly's official bill record, SB 26-130 was **Postponed Indefinitely (killed) by the Senate Health & Human Services Committee on 2026-04-23 on a 7-0 vote**; the bill's status is now **"Lost."** **This corrects prior cycles (2026-06-08, 2026-06-15), which kept the bill on watch as "introduced / not signed / effective 2026-08-12 if enacted." The bill never advanced past its first committee — the postponement happened on 2026-04-23, before those cycles ran — and the 2026-08-12 conditional effective date is moot.** For substance/context only (not in force): the bill would have classified certain med-spa prescription-drug practices as **unfair or deceptive trade practices under the Colorado Consumer Protection Act** — e.g., failing to designate a licensed health-care provider with prescriptive authority for clinical oversight, and failing to create/maintain/produce records of serious adverse events — with enforcement available to the **Colorado Attorney General or local district attorneys**, not solely professional licensing boards. **Net practice impact**: no new Colorado deceptive-trade-practice obligation arises from SB 26-130 in the 2026 session. The pre-existing Colorado baseline (HB 1024 prominent-display, SB 26-189 AI-deployer framework) stands unchanged. Off active watch until a refiling appears in a future session. (Distinct from SB 26-189, the AI-deployer law, below — that one is enacted.)
- **Skill hook**: `cancellation-no-show-policy-author` (CO prominent-display flag, HB 1024); `membership-program-builder` (CO compliance checklist item); `ai-consent-and-compliance-guardrails` (CO display rule for AI-generated notices **plus the SB 26-189 HIPAA-covered-vs-not gate for AI hiring and financing tools, effective January 1, 2027**); future `operations/stylist-hiring-rubric` (CO AI-hiring-tool deployer duties if the rubric is automated).

### Indiana
- **SB 282 — SIGNED INTO LAW (status change, 2026-06-08 monitor cycle)**: Governor Mike Braun signed SB 282 on **March 5, 2026** (previously logged as "introduced"). Two distinct effective dates:
  - **Most operational provisions take effect July 1, 2026.**
  - **Board registration framework must be established by October 1, 2026 (added 2026-06-29 monitor cycle)** — the Indiana Medical Licensing Board (with the Board of Pharmacy) must publish the formal facility-registration procedures by **October 1, 2026**. This is the intermediate milestone between the July 1, 2026 operational go-live and the January 1, 2027 registration deadline: operators cannot complete registration until the Board publishes the mechanics, expected on or before October 1, 2026.
  - **Registration deadline: January 1, 2027** — med spas have until then to register with the Indiana Medical Licensing Board. **Non-compliance penalty (added 2026-06-29 monitor cycle): operating without a valid registration after January 1, 2027 is unlawful and carries a fine of up to $5,000.**
  - Substance (now enacted): mandatory med-spa registration; designation of a responsible practitioner present for a "sufficient amount of time"; adverse-event reporting to the Board no later than **fifteen (15) days** after a serious adverse event (with the prescription medication involved and relevant records). **Correction (2026-06-22 monitor cycle): a prior cycle logged this window as "5 days." The engrossed/enacted bill text reads "not later than fifteen (15) days after the occurrence of a patient's serious adverse event" — the correct figure is 15 days.** Some secondary blog summaries still report "5 days" (apparently carried over from earlier proposed drafts or conflated with the Iowa proposal); the enacted Indiana figure is 15 days. The precise definition of a "serious adverse event" and the exact notice mechanics are being finalized in Medical Licensing Board rulemaking.

### Iowa
- **HSB 591 — Medical Spa Oversight Act** (introduced 2026; **status: proposed / not enacted** — re-checked 2026-07-20: advanced out of subcommittee to full House committee Feb 2026 with a recommendation of amendment and passage, but **no signed law surfaced and the 2026 session produced no enactment**. Treat as **not-enacted for the 2026 session**; whether it carries over or is refiled in a future session is unconfirmed. Verify before relying on it — do not let repeated "still proposed" answers drift into an implied "still alive"):
  - Medical directors must be within **60 miles** of the delegated service location.
  - Medical directors must provide at least **4 hours of direct, on-site supervision per week**.
  - These are hard rules, not guidelines.
  - **Additional oversight provisions surfaced 2026-07-06 monitor cycle** (added detail, same proposed bill): the Act would require any facility operating as a medical spa — or providing wellness services, or storing/administering/dispensing/distributing prescription drugs — to be **licensed by the Iowa Board of Medicine**. "Wellness services" is drawn broadly (e.g., Botox, laser hair removal, skin-pigmentation treatment, microneedling, IV nutrient therapy, IM vitamin/nutrient injections). Each spa must designate a **"responsible person"** licensed under Iowa Code **Chapter 148** (Medicine & Surgery) or **Chapter 155A** (Pharmacy) to ensure compliance. The license application gives the Board the right to **inspect the facility and audit services**, and the Board must maintain a **public database of medical spas**. **Serious-adverse-event reporting window: within 5 days** of the event (contrast Indiana's 15-day window — see the Indiana note; the "5-day" figure that some blogs mis-attribute to Indiana appears to trace to this Iowa proposal). Administrative penalties: **up to $500 per violation**. No proposed effective date is specified; the Board of Medicine would adopt implementing rules.
- **Skill hook**: `ai-consent-and-compliance-guardrails` (IA supervision rule for any med spa with a remote medical director arrangement; if HSB 591 is enacted, add the responsible-person Chapter-148/155A gate and the 5-day adverse-event line); flag in `cancellation-no-show-policy-author` med-spa compliance footer if state = Iowa. **No skill file hardcodes these figures — directional for the next skill-evaluator pass; do not wire in until the bill is confirmed enacted.**

### Texas
- **Ownership**: Physicians only may own the medical entity. Medical director must be genuinely involved (not name-only).
- **SB 1188 — healthcare AI disclosure (effective 2025-09-01; added 2026-07-13 monitor cycle)**: A health-care practitioner who uses AI **for diagnostic purposes** — including AI-generated recommendations for diagnosis or treatment based on a patient's records — **must disclose that use to the patient** (verbally or in writing). The practitioner must act within their licensure scope and **review AI-generated records** per Texas Medical Board standards. Enforcement: board discipline (up to license suspension/revocation) and AG civil penalties reported in the **$5,000–$250,000 per violation** range. The law also carries EHR data-localization provisions.
  - **Scope note — read carefully**: this duty attaches to **diagnostic/treatment** use of AI, **not** to a booking chatbot. A TX med spa's **AI receptionist that only books appointments is outside SB 1188**. It fires when the practice uses **AI skin/hair analysis, AI-assisted treatment planning, or an AI charting/diagnostic assistant** on a clinical-tier service — an increasingly common med-spa purchase, and the reason this belongs in the KB. Do not over-claim it as a general chatbot-disclosure mandate.
- **TRAIGA / HB 149 (effective 2026-01-01)**: Texas's general AI governance act. Its **AI-interaction disclosure duty binds government agencies** (and healthcare practitioners via SB 1188) — it is **not** a general private-sector chatbot-disclosure mandate, and prior drafts of this KB entry overstated it. What does carry across: where a disclosure is required, it must be **clear, conspicuous, plain-language, and not obscured by a dark pattern**. Useful as a drafting standard even where not strictly binding.
- **SB 140 — telemarketing / text messages (effective 2025-09-01)**: **Not an AI law** (see the sourcing warning in the cross-state AI-disclosure entry above). It expands the Texas Telephone Solicitation Act to cover **text, graphic, and image messages**, requires seller **registration with the Secretary of State** (registration fee, surety bond, periodic reports), and creates **DTPA private-right-of-action** exposure reported at up to **$1,500 per violation plus treble damages**. This matters to salons for a different reason than AI: **any TX salon or spa running SMS marketing is potentially a covered telephone solicitor.** Route to counsel on whether the practice's SMS program triggers registration.
- **Skill hook**: `ai-consent-and-compliance-guardrails` (TX physician-ownership hard gate; **TX SB 1188 diagnostic-AI disclosure gate** where the practice uses AI skin/hair analysis or AI treatment planning); `sales/sms-campaign-builder` (**TX SB 140 telemarketing-registration flag** for TX SMS programs — new, and unrelated to AI); `operations/ai-receptionist-disclosure-and-escalation` (a booking-only bot is **outside** SB 1188 — the skill discloses on best-practice/EU/CA grounds, not on a claimed TX mandate).
